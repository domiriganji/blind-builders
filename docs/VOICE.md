# Voice and tone

This guide covers every word a member reads on Blind Creators Studio: pages, buttons, hints, errors, flash notices, job progress, empty states, guides, and what the AI writes, since the AI writes much of what members read. It also covers the system prompts that tell the AI how to speak.

It comes from four blind and low-vision reviewers who read the whole site: a disability advocate, a blind developer, a member who became blind late in life and speaks English as a second language, and a deafblind braille reader. It also draws on the community voices in `docs/research/landscape-brief.md` section 3, including Robert Kingett's "The Disability Dongle" and Andrew Leland's warnings.

---

## 1. Who we are talking to

- **Makers.** Blind and low-vision adults who come here to build websites and apps, write, publish, research and get things done. They are the authors and the experts on their own access. Nobody here is an audience for someone else's kindness.
- **People who read in different ways.** Some listen to speech. Some read a braille display, often 40 cells at a time. Some use magnification, zoom and large text. Some use more than one of these. Some are deafblind. Some have useful sight.
- **People with different stories.** Some were born blind and never "lost" anything. Some became blind at 60. Some have low vision that changes from day to day. Some are senior developers and some have never written code.
- **Many readers of second-language English.** Plain words and short sentences help everyone. Idioms leave some readers out.

## 2. How we want them to feel

When a member closes the page, they should feel:

1. **Respected as an adult and a maker.** They are not pitied, praised for ordinary things, protected from facts, or talked down to.
2. **In control.** Nothing happens behind their back. Their words stay theirs. They decide what to share and what to disclose.
3. **Told the truth.** Every claim says who made it and what was checked. Nothing is dressed up.
4. **Safe when something fails.** They know what happened, that it was not their fault (when it was not), what was kept, and what to do next.
5. **Part of a community of peers.** Help comes from members who answer when they can. Thanks go to people who give their time.

The test for every sentence: would a blind or low-vision member who is tired, rushed and reading by braille or speech feel respected by it, and could they act on it?

## 3. What members love: keep it, word for word

The reviewers singled these out. Do not "improve" them away.

**Makers, not an audience**
- "Your idea, in your words" / "Your request, in your words" / "What you added or corrected"
- "Your words stay yours. The AI never silently changes what you wrote."
- "Restoring never loses your current text: it is kept as a version too."
- "Tools that blind and low-vision people made for each other" and "Made by a blind or low-vision person."
- "Say it the way you would tell a friend: who it is for, what it should do, and what it should say."
- "It finds, explains and prepares. You stay in control and do the last step yourself."
- "Only the ones you tick are built. None are ticked to start with."

**The barrier belongs to the tool, not the person**
- "You are asking them to fix their barrier, not for a favour." (publishing-papers.md)
- "Known accessibility problems: The page has a picture CAPTCHA with no audio option."
- "Never use blindfolded sighted people as stand-ins for blind participants" (research prompt)
- "If the AI tells you to press a button you cannot find, it may not be there." (The fault sits with the AI.)

**Honesty as respect**
- "Written by the AI. The site has not checked these claims."
- "This is the AI's opinion, not a check by the site."
- "Computed by the site, not the AI."
- "Nobody on this site tested the tool. It is not a member report."
- "What was checked: nothing."
- "The AI did not find reports of problems. That does not mean there are none."
- "Something went wrong on our side. Your work so far is saved."
- "Nothing new was started, so you are not charged twice." / "You will not pay twice." / "Credits do not expire."
- "Anyone who has your key can spend your money."
- "With credits, you pay the AI price plus 25 percent, which pays for running the site."

**Skepticism as skill**
- "Check with evidence, not by looking."
- "12 blind spreadsheet users never fully trusted AI output without checking it"

**Blindness built in, not added later**
- "Spelled out, one character at a time: L, W, S, Q; ..." and "or write it in braille."
- "Each changed line starts with the word Added or Removed." and "Changed index.html: 1 line added, 1 line removed."
- "The taps are a rhythm on one note, not a tune, so you do not need to tell pitches apart."
- "Write or paste your text in a plain text box that works with screen readers and braille displays."
- "Add your braille display if you used one." and "The answer stays still while you read it with your screen reader or braille display. Nothing jumps, repeats or disappears."
- "Text in square brackets, like [your passport number], is a gap for you to fill in yourself."
- "Whole answers, summary first."

**Sighted helpers serve the blind maker**
- "The member may not see the page, so say what effect something has, not only what it looks like." ("May" and "not only" are what make it work: describe the effect, and colour is still allowed.)

**Plain adult errors with an example**
- "Use 80 characters or fewer for your name."
- "Enter a whole email address, like name@example.com."
- "The email or password is not right. Check both and try again."
- "Say which update it broke after, for example NVDA 2026.1 or iOS 27. Write 'not sure' if you do not know."

**Control, consent and gentle exits**
- "Keep my account and go back", "Keep it and go back", "No, keep this project"
- "Prefer not to say", "You do not have to say.", "Only give what you are happy to share."

**Warmth without pity**
- "Thank you for checking.", "Thank you for helping.", "Thank you for letting other members know."
- "Nobody has volunteered yet. You could be the first."
- "Members answer when they can."
- "Usable with workarounds means you got your work done, but only with extra steps or tools."

**Blind experts quoted as authorities**
- "Kelly Ford, a screen reader user for decades", "Taylor Arndt, a blind developer who tests apps made with AI", "Jamal Mazrui, a blind developer and author". Quote them for what they know. Never use them as inspiration.

**Everyday sight words, used naturally**
- "see whether it already exists", "See the changed lines", "How it looks", "Fixed before you saw it". Blind people say see, look and watch all day.

**Button names that make sense on their own**
- "This helped me (answer 2, from Member C14D)", "Delete errand: Renew my library card online" (to become "Delete request: ..."), "Accept suggestion 1", "Check and save my key", "Send my idea to the AI", "Mark as answered".

## 4. Rules

Every rule comes with a check you can run and a before-and-after example from the site.

### 4.1 Speak to an adult and a fellow maker

Do not pity, do not praise ordinary tasks, and do not coach. Assume competence.

**Never write these in site text or AI answers:** despite, brave, inspiring, inspirational, overcome, suffer / suffering from, afflicted, victim, special / special needs, visually impaired, visually challenged, differently abled, handicapped, "the blind" (as a noun), "people like you", Oops, Great job, Great question, Well done (for ordinary tasks).

**Words that talk down:** simply, just (when it means "this is trivial"), easy, obviously, carefully (as an instruction), properly / proper, really (as in "what really happened"), don't worry, "you do not need to know", "for your safety" (as padding).

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Before the AI builds something ... a read-back. Listen to it carefully." | "... a read-back. Check it before you tell the AI to go ahead." |
| "You do not need to know how websites are made. Plain words are fine." | "Plain words work. Technical terms work too." |
| "Write what really happened." | "Write what happened, in your own words." |
| "Test any pattern with a proper analysis before you report it." | "Test any pattern with a statistical analysis before you report it." |
| "You have a recovery code. For your safety it is never shown again." | "You have a recovery code. It is never shown again, so nobody using your account can read it." |

Check: search member-facing strings, guides and system prompts for every word in the two lists above.

### 4.2 Name blindness plainly, include low vision, and only when it matters

- Say "blind" and "low vision". Where the reader could be either, write "blind and low-vision members", "blind or low-vision person", or use "members".
- Never "other blind members" (it tells a low-vision reader they are blind) and never "people like you".
- Do not open a page with what members do not do ("without needing to see", "without sight"). Open with what they make.
- In the rare case that a source uses "visually impaired", quote it as the source's term in quotation marks.
- Ask about history in words that fit someone born blind: "Blind or low vision since", never "when you lost sight" or "when your sight changed". Ask about low vision as a separate optional question, not as an answer to "when".

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Build your ideas with AI, without needing to see the screen" | "Build your ideas with AI: websites, apps, writing and research" |
| "Ask other blind members to test your website or app with their screen reader" | "Ask blind and low-vision members to test your website or app with their own screen reader, braille display or magnifier" |
| "whether it worked for blind people like you" | "whether it worked for other members" |
| "Lost sight later in life" | "Later in life" (under the label "Blind or low vision since") |
| "Understanding charts and data without sight" | "Charts and data by numbers, sound, braille and touch" |
| "written by a 90 percent blind operator" | "written by an operator who is 90 percent blind" |

Check: search for `other blind members`, `like you`, `lost sight`, `without sight`, `without needing to see`, `visually impaired`, `blind person made`, `blind people made`.

### 4.3 Sensory words: everyday sight words stay, and output is "read", not "heard"

Two rules apply together. Over-correcting is its own kind of condescension, and assuming speech leaves out braille readers.

1. **Keep see, look and watch** as everyday words. "See whether it already exists" and "How it looks" are fine. Do not change them to "hear".
2. **Do not assume the member listens.** When the text is about how a member takes in the site's or a tool's output, write read, check, find or go to, not listen, hear, heard, spoken or "talks over". Where speech really is the subject, add the braille side: "read or hear", "says or shows", "in speech or braille".
3. **Name all three ways of reading as equals:** speech, braille, magnification or zoom. Where a form asks about the member's tools, invite braille displays and magnifiers too.
4. **Every sound has a silent equivalent stated on the same page.** This covers the job taps, the terminal bell, sonification and sounds the AI adds to games. Say what carries the same news: focus moving to a link, a status message, a visual bell, a text or braille mode.
5. **Claim only what is real.** The site sends no vibration, so do not promise it. The job page does not announce each step, so do not say it "says the current step".
6. **Say "choose", not "press"**, for buttons and links. "Choose" fits keyboards, routing keys, touch and voice.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Listen for three things:" | "Check three things:" |
| "What did you hear first when the page opened? Type it from memory." | "What did your screen reader say or show first when the page opened? Type it from memory." |
| "Nothing streams or talks over your screen reader." | "Nothing streams, talks over your speech or moves your braille display while you read." |
| "Sounds use rhythm, not pitch, so they work with hearing loss and can be felt as vibration." | "The three taps are a rhythm on one note, not a tune. If you cannot hear them, you miss nothing: focus moves to a link to your result, in speech, in braille or on screen." |
| "Is what you type spoken?" | "Is what you type echoed?" |
| "Press Try again, or reword your request." | "Choose Try again, or reword your request." |

Check: search templates, guides and prompts with `listen|hear|heard|spoken|speaks|talks over|vibrat|Press `. Every hit must either be about sound itself or have "or read" or "or braille" beside it.

### 4.4 Looks and colours: the effect by default, colours when the member wants them

The organizer decided this after reading the reviewers' views. Some blind members understand colours and want them; others would feel bad being told or asked about something they do not experience. So:

- **By default, describe how something looks by its effect:** "looks calm and finished, easy to read on a phone". Do not name colours, colour codes or fonts, and never ask a member to choose colours.
- **A member who wants colours gets them.** In Your profile, "The effect, then the main colours in plain words" turns them on ("deep green and cream"). A member who mentions colours themselves, for example a brand colour in their idea, has asked: follow their wishes and talk about colours freely. Colour codes and font names come only when asked.
- **Never hide anything else about a member's own work.** What failed, why, and what the AI found are always said, in effect words.
- **Never give the reason as the member's sight.** Not "which this page does not show", not "because you cannot see". If a member chose the effect only and the AI's words name colours anyway, say so neutrally and say how to read them.
- Prompts never say "the person cannot see". They say "may not see", and the shared member note (4.18) carries the member's choice.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "The AI described the look by its colours, which this page does not show. Its opinion is below." | "The AI's description of the look is about its colours, so it is not shown, as you chose in Your profile. To read descriptions with colours, change that choice." |
| "The AI found something on this page that may be hard for visitors to see or read." | Effect only: "Some text on the menu is faint, so prices are hard to read on a phone." With colours on: "Grey text on the white menu is faint, so prices are hard to read on a phone." |
| Prompt: "Never name colours ... the person cannot see them." | Prompt: the shared member note's looks line, which follows the member's profile choice. |

Check: no prompt says "the person cannot see", and no member text gives sight as the reason something is not shown.

### 4.5 Put the barrier in the tool, not the person

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "nearly one in five could not do a literature review or evaluate visual outputs on their own" | "for nearly one in five, the tools they had did not let them do a literature review or check visual outputs on their own" |
| "vague image descriptions made them give up" | "vague image descriptions were where their questions most often hit a dead end" |
| "There are 3 numbered sources, but the draft also cites these numbers. Remove them or find a real source." | "The AI cited these numbers, but they are not in the list of 3 sources. Remove them, or add a real source for each one." |

### 4.6 Lead with what is there, then what is not

Say who helps first, then what is missing. Never open with "no".

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "There is no phone line. Help comes from other members in Ask the community." | "Help comes from other members in Ask the community. There is no phone line." |
| "There are no staff: the library keeps working because members help each other." | "The library keeps working because members help each other. There are no staff." |

Do not console. "You are not alone in this" is comfort nobody asked for. State who helps and how.

### 4.7 Disclosure belongs to the member

- Nothing the AI writes for other readers (a website, a cover letter, a paper, a prepared form answer) mentions that the member is blind or has low vision, or how they read, unless they ask.
- When the AI edits a member's writing, it never changes the words they use about disability or about themselves ("blind", "disabled"), and never suggests euphemisms for them.
- Do not assume a blind researcher studies blindness. Add blind-participant advice only when the research question involves blind people.

### 4.8 Honesty over comfort

- Keep every statement of what was or was not checked, what it costs, and what cannot be undone. A warmer rewrite never removes a fact.
- A page never contradicts itself. Do not list contrast as "Not checked" on a page that says "Passed: Text stands out clearly". Do not tell a new member a password "cannot be reset" when a recovery code is coming next.
- Show the mechanism instead of praising yourself. Not "Every check is recorded honestly", but "Every check is saved in the checker's own words, in a public record anyone can verify."
- Promise only what happens. Write "Members answer when they can", not "Other members will read your question and answer it". If a preference is saved but the AI does not use it, say so next to the question.
- Offer only options that exist today. Do not show "two ways" when one is not on sale.
- Say what actually happens, not what does not. "If you have neither, the page tells you plainly, and the AI does not run." is better than "Nothing is faked."
- Avoid "Nobody at the site has checked it", which suggests staff who chose not to. Write "Nobody has checked it."

### 4.9 Errors and refusals

**Shape.** Say what happened, then whether anything was lost, saved or charged, then what to do now. No message ends without a next step. Name the fault-holder when it is the site or the AI: "on our side", "the AI made a mistake". Only say "nothing was saved" or "nothing was charged" where the code confirms it.

**No blame words pointing at the member:** wrong tries, invalid, rejected, declined, malformed, "fix the problems", "fix it" (when nothing is broken), "too much".

**Form errors.**
- Page title and h1: "Please check 1 answer: Sign in" / "Please check 2 answers: Sign in" (the count comes first, with no blame), not "Sign in: please fix the problems".
- Summary heading: "What to check" (not "There is a problem"). The h1 just above it already gives the count, so the summary does not say it again. Its list has one link for each answer to check.
- With JavaScript, focus moves to the summary and the page stops marking it as an alert just before, so a screen reader reads it once. Without JavaScript it stays an alert.
- Hidden field prefix: "Please check: " (not "Error: "). Screen readers already announce "invalid" for marked fields.
- Each field message names the field and the fix, with an example.

**Secrets refusals.** Use one wording everywhere, from `studio/sensitive.py`, plus the page's own reason where it applies. The wording must allow for the detector being wrong, because accessibility reports say "PIN" and "password" all the time:

> This looks like it includes a password, PIN, code, card number or API key, so it was not saved or sent to the AI. Please take it out: this site never needs them. If it is not one, change the wording a little, for example "the PIN box is not announced", and send it again.

Where the text would be public, add: "Everyone can read this page."

**AI refusals.** "The AI would not do this request. This can happen by mistake, for example with words about passwords or PINs. Try saying it another way. If you think it should work, ask in Ask the community."

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Too many wrong tries. Wait 15 minutes and try again. This protects your account from people guessing." | "Several sign-in tries did not work, so sign-in is paused for 15 minutes to protect your account from guessing. Your account and your work are unchanged. Please try again after that." |
| "Your Claude API key was rejected. Check it on your account page." | "Anthropic did not accept your Claude API key, so nothing was done. The key may have been deleted or changed in the Claude Console (platform.claude.com). Please connect it again on Your account." |
| "The AI's answer was cut off or malformed. Please try again." | "The AI's answer came back cut off or incomplete, so nothing was saved. Please try again." |
| "The AI made files the site cannot accept, so nothing was saved. The file name "/x" starts with a slash..." | "The AI made a mistake in the files, so nothing was saved. Please try again. If it happens again, ask for something smaller. Technical detail: ..." |
| "This looks like a card number, a password or a one-time code. Never put those here. Remove it and try again." | The shared secrets message above. |

### 4.10 Empty states

Say what is not there yet in a short sentence, without a sad count, then offer the next step if there is one. Say it once per page.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "0 members found this helpful." | "No member has marked this helpful yet." |
| "0 members are helping keep it working." | "No volunteers yet." |
| "This deletes Rivers of Africa quiz, your idea and all 0 versions." | "This deletes Rivers of Africa quiz and your idea. Nothing has been built yet. This cannot be undone." |
| "No reports yet.Latest report: none yet" | "No reports yet." |

Keep "What was checked: nothing." It is an honest record, not an empty state. "Nothing yet" would promise a check that may never come.

### 4.11 Progress and jobs

- One H1 that says what is happening, in words that make sense in a list of jobs: "Reading back your idea: Rivers of Africa quiz", not "Reading Rivers of Africa quiz".
- Say how the member will know it is done, in every sense: "When it is done, focus moves to a link to your result. With sound on, you also hear three short taps."
- On failure, use "Something went wrong on our side. Your work so far is saved." and add the next step: "Please try again. If it stops at the same step again, tell members in Ask the community which step it was."

### 4.12 Results and AI-written labels

Every result says who made it and what was checked, in the existing plain pattern ("Written by the AI. The site has not checked these claims." / "Computed by the site, not the AI." / "This is the AI's opinion, not a check by the site."). Keep these labels on every AI result. Never soften them.

In before-and-after suggestions, the member's words come first and the AI's words are clearly only a suggestion: "Your words:" and "Suggested:", not "Was:" and "Now:". "Now" sounds as if the change has already been made.

A suggestion that no longer fits after the member edited their own text is information, not an error. Show it as a notice, not an alert.

### 4.13 Length, front-loading and plain English

- The outcome comes first, then the reason, then the next step.
- Aim for about 20 words a sentence at most, with one idea each. Split long paragraphs, such as tool research summaries of 80 to 130 words, into a plain lead sentence followed by short sentences or a list.
- Say each fact once per page. Do not repeat a status under the H1 and again in the section, and do not repeat "optional" in a hint when the label says it.
- **No idioms.** Avoid: for good, add up, the final word, wins over, lands (for "arrives"), safety rails, too good to be true, costly to disprove.
- **Explain jargon the first time, or use a plain word:** API key ("a private code from Anthropic that lets this site use the AI and send the bill to you"), UTC, zip ("one zip file, a packed folder"), sandbox ("a protected space"), web host, escaped ("handled"), rate limit and malformed (drop them). Keep exact technical terms where an expert needs them, in brackets after the plain words.
- **Dates:** use one format everywhere, "13 October 2026". Give times as "12:29 UTC" only where the time matters. The first mention on a page explains UTC: "UTC, the same as London time in winter".
- Avoid "cheap", which can sound like poor quality or poor people. Say "low-cost".

### 4.14 One name for each thing

| Thing | Name | Not |
|---|---|---|
| Community Q&A section | Ask the community | Help |
| A member's public label | code name (for example Member BDF4) | member name |
| A Do this for me item | request | errand |
| A member's history question | Blind or low vision since | Sight, when your sight changed, lost sight |
| Account page | Your account | your account page (in links) |

The sight question and its choices must be the same on the profile, the Find and Library report forms, and public report lines.

### 4.15 Warmth without pity

- Thank people for what they give: a check, an answer, a report, a tool.
- Say "Please" when we ask the member to do something because of us (wait, take something out, connect again). Ordinary steps need no "please".
- Say "Sorry" once, plainly, only where the site's own limits cost the member something real. One example is being locked out with no recovery code. Never apologise out of habit.
- Reassure only with a fact the site can stand behind ("Your account and your work are unchanged"). Do not add comfort words ("don't worry", "you are not alone").
- Do not make members feel guilty for using help. Write "Then members know they can help someone else.", not "so members can spend their time on other questions".
- A short goodbye is fine when someone leaves: "Thank you for being part of this community."

### 4.16 Choices and consent

- Everything personal is optional and offers "Prefer not to say".
- Defaults never do anything risky or costly. Nothing is pre-ticked, and deleting is never the easiest choice.
- Read back before acting, and keep the member's own words visible.
- If the site asks for a preference, it uses it. Profile answers (tools, answer style, language, how looks are described) reach every AI prompt through the shared member note (4.18).

### 4.17 Keep braille lines clean

- Put a space or full stop between parts that share a line or a table cell, so "yet.Latest" and "you3,000" never fuse.
- Make sure an AI step ends with a full stop before a following "Screen reader tip:".
- Avoid decorative punctuation in page titles. Use a comma rather than an en dash between page and site name.
- Put long codes (64-character fingerprints) at the end of a line, after a short label that says what they are for.
- Use words over lone symbols: "Added" and "Removed", not "+" and "-".

### 4.18 How the AI is told to speak

Every system prompt (build, errands, find, learn, research, write, ai-tools) starts with the same shared member note. It is one constant in `studio/ai.py` (`MEMBER_VOICE`), filled from the member's profile by `member_voice(user)`, and `AIClient` puts it before every system prompt by itself, so section prompts never repeat it. It reads, in short:

> The person you work for is blind or has low vision. They may read with a screen reader in speech or braille, with magnification or zoom, or with some sight. Their tools: {screen_reader or "not given"}. Their answer style: {verbosity}. Treat them as a capable adult, a maker and the expert on their own access. Be warm, calm and direct. Do not pity them, do not praise ordinary things, and do not apologise or reassure out of habit. Never write despite, brave, inspiring, overcome, suffer, special needs, visually impaired, visually challenged or differently abled, and never write simply, just, easy, obviously or do not worry. Mention blindness only when it matters to the answer. Words like see and look are fine, but do not assume they listen: write read, check or go to, not "you will hear". Do not explain how screen readers work unless asked. Many readers speak English as a second language: use plain words and no idioms. If something failed, say what happened, whether anything was lost, and what they can do next. Never claim something was checked or tested unless it was. Put the summary first. Use short sentences and short headings. No tables unless asked, no emoji, no decorative characters or ASCII art. Keep list items short, with the key word first.

It also carries the disclosure rule below and the looks line from the member's profile (4.4). Section prompts add these where they apply:
- **Anything written for other readers** (built sites, cover letters, papers, prepared form answers): "Never mention the person's blindness or low vision, or how they read, in anything meant for other readers, unless they ask."
- **Editing the member's writing:** "Respect the writer's voice. Never change the words the writer uses about disability or about themselves, such as blind or disabled, and never suggest euphemisms for them."
- **Describing looks:** nothing to add; the shared note carries the member's choice (4.4).
- **Building with sound:** "Every sound must also give the same news as text, for example in the role=status message. Never use sound alone."

## 6. How we settled the reviewers' disagreements

- **Colours.** All four reviewers objected to a blanket ban on colours, because some members understand and want them. The organizer decided: the effect only by default, and nobody is asked to choose colours; members who want colours turn them on in Your profile or mention colours themselves, and then get them freely (4.4).
- **Sight words.** See, look and watch stay. Listen and hear are replaced only when they assume how a member reads output. This answers both the over-correction warning and the braille readers.
- **Sight history.** The label is "Blind or low vision since", not "When your sight changed", because someone born blind had no change. Low vision is a separate optional question.
- **Warmth.** Thanks are always welcome. "Sorry" is said once, only where the site's limits cost something real. Reassurance must be a checkable fact. Name the cause ("the AI made a mistake") instead of adding "this was not caused by you". No consolation headings.
- **Home heading.** It leads with what members make, not with sight.
- **Form errors.** "Please check 1 answer: Sign in" puts the count first without blame.
