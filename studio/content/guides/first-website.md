## The short version

1. Open [Build](/build) and describe the website in your own words.
2. Check the read-back. Correct it, or tell the AI to go ahead.
3. Wait on the job page. Use "Check again", or wait for the taps and the link to your result.
4. Read the result page: what was built, what the site checked, and what nobody has checked yet.
5. Open the preview and move through your site with your screen reader, braille display or magnifier.
6. Ask for one change at a time.
7. Ask for a peer check, so another member tests it with their own screen reader, braille display or magnifier.

## Before you start: what to say

The AI builds from what you tell it, so a good description saves time. Say:

- What the website is for, in one sentence. For example: "A website for my cake shop, so people can see what I bake and how to order."
- Who will use it.
- Which pages it needs. For example: home, cakes and prices, how to order, contact.
- The words that must appear exactly: your business name, opening hours, prices, address.
- Anything it must not have. For example: no pictures that only show text, no music that plays by itself.

What can be built here: websites, phone web apps, small tools and games made of web pages. They have no server, so they cannot take payments, send email or messages, or keep accounts with sign-in. Anything they save stays in the visitor's own browser. If your idea needs one of these, the read-back should say so and describe the closest thing that can be built. If it does not, ask.

Plain words work. Technical terms work too.

## Step 1: describe it and check the read-back

Open [Build](/build) and type or dictate your description. The AI does not build anything yet. First it reads back what it understood: the purpose, the pages, and what goes on each one.

Check three things:

- Is anything missing that you asked for?
- Did it add anything you did not ask for?
- Are names, prices and times exactly right?

If anything is wrong, say so in your own words. When the read-back is right, tell it to go ahead.

## Step 2: wait for the build

Building takes a little time, so you get a job page. Its heading starts with "Working on it". Activate "Check again" to see if it is done, or, if JavaScript is on, wait: your focus moves to a link to the result, with three short taps if sound is on. You can leave the page and come back through [Your recent work](/jobs).

## Step 3: read the result

The result page puts the summary first. It tells you:

- what was built, or what changed since the last version;
- what the site checked automatically, and the result of each check;
- what the site could not check, and still needs a person.

## Step 4: open the preview

The result page has a link to open the preview of your website. Explore it the way a visitor would:

- Jump through the headings. Is there exactly one level 1 heading, and does it say what the page is?
- Tab through every link and button. Does each one say what it does when you read or hear it on its own?
- Fill in any form. Does every field have a label? If you leave a required field empty, are you told what is wrong?
- Check the page title your screen reader gives when the page loads.

The preview runs separately from this site, so a mistake in your website cannot read your sign-in to this site.

## Step 5: understand the checks

The site checks each version in two ways. The result page shows both.

### Checks on the code

The site reads the HTML of each page. These checks look for:

- a language set on the page, and a page title;
- exactly one main heading, and headings that do not skip levels;
- text alternatives on images;
- labels on form fields, and names on buttons and links;
- no repeated ids, no blocked zooming, and a main region;
- titles on frames, no forced tab order, no page that refreshes by itself;
- header cells on data tables, no sound that plays by itself;
- a page light enough for slow phones.

### Checks in a browser

The site also opens each page in a browser, on a phone and on a computer, and checks how it looks on screen. On the result page this is under "How it looks on screen". These checks look for:

- nothing that makes the page scroll sideways;
- text that stands out clearly from what is behind it, so it is easy to read;
- no text that is cut off, and no text on top of other text;
- buttons, links and form fields big enough to tap on a phone;
- text big enough to read on a phone;
- every picture loading.

An AI also looks at pictures of the pages and says what it notices. That is its opinion, and the result page labels it as one.

### What no check covers

The checks do not run a screen reader. They do not check how a real screen reader reads the page, in speech or in braille, zooming to 400 percent on a phone, or whether buttons do what their names say. That is why peer checks exist.

Taylor Arndt, a blind developer who tests apps made with AI, keeps finding the same problems: buttons announced only as "button", pages with no headings, and focus that never moves after an action ([Taylor Arndt](https://taylorarndt.substack.com/p/everybody-is-vibe-coding-here-is)). Check for these in your preview.

## Step 6: ask for a change

Ask for one change at a time, so each one is quick to check. For example:

- "Put the opening hours in a list, one day per line."
- "Put our phone number and address at the top of the contact page."
- "The menu button only says 'Menu'. Make it say 'Show the list of cakes'."

The AI makes the change straight away, without a read-back, so say exactly what you want. When it is done, the result page tells you what changed. The site works this out itself by comparing the two versions, so it does not depend on what the AI says it did. The checks run again on the new version. Go to the changed part in the preview and try it.

If a change made things worse, say what went wrong in plain words, the same way you would tell a person, or open the earlier version and ask for the change from there.

## Step 7: ask for a peer check

Machine checks and the AI cannot tell you what a screen reader user experiences. Kelly Ford, a screen reader user for decades who built an email app with AI, put it this way: "AI can implement accessibility patterns. Only a screen reader user can verify that they actually work." ([Kelly Ford's QuickMail story (source)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kellylford/QuickMail/main/docs/planning/the-quickmail-story.md))

In [Peer checks](/checks), ask blind and low-vision members to test your website with their own screen reader, braille display or magnifier and device. Say what you want them to try, for example "order a cake from start to finish". Each check is saved in the checker's own words, including what they could not test, in a public record anyone can verify. When you can, check someone else's work too. That is how the community keeps working without staff.

## When you are ready to share it

- Read the summary of the latest version once more.
- Make sure every peer check finding you care about is fixed.
- Then share the link with the people it is for.

If you get stuck at any step, [ask a question about this guide](/help/ask?title=Your%20first%20website%20with%20Build%2C%20step%20by%20step).
